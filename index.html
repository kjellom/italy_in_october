Who this is for

Two travelers, one long way to meet

One of us is flying in from Los Angeles, the other from Bangkok — Rome is where the two routes actually cross, so that's where the trip starts. From there we're not chasing a new hotel every second night. We wanted one real home on the Sorrentine Peninsula, a car, and enough time to let the same coastline become familiar rather than photographed once and left behind.

We're after the quieter version of this coast: a village where the restaurants cook for the people who live there, day trips to the places worth the crowds, and evenings with nowhere particular to be.

The region, briefly

Where Rome hands off to the sea

This stretch of Italy is layered rather than linear. Rome sits at the center of an empire's worth of ruins, still lived in, still a functioning capital of nearly three million people. An hour and a half south, the Bay of Naples is dominated by Vesuvius — the volcano that buried Pompeii and Herculaneum in 79 CE and still stands directly over the city today. Further along, the Sorrentine Peninsula splits two seas from a single ridge of lemon terraces and olive groves, and the Amalfi Coast unfolds along a single cliff road that connects fishing villages that spent most of history more interested in each other than in the rest of the world.

Greek colonists, Roman emperors, medieval maritime republics, and Grand Tour poets all passed through here in some order — the ground rarely lets you forget it.

Why here, and why now

What late October and November actually bring

Working in our favor

  • Daytime temperatures still comfortable, typically the 60s–70s°F on the coast through late October
  • Summer crowds gone — the coast road, the ruins, and the ferries are all easier to move through
  • Lemon and olive harvest season on the peninsula — the groves are working, not decorative
  • Clear autumn light, especially useful for the coastline and the ruins alike
  • Lower prices and far more room to actually get a table

Working against us

  • November 1 is the traditional end of season here — many smaller restaurants and hotels close around then, independent of the actual weather
  • Capri ferries stop entirely on October 31 — a hard cutoff, not a soft one
  • Rain becomes more likely as November goes on, and daylight keeps shrinking
  • Some of the smallest Amalfi Coast villages thin out fast after the season ends — worth confirming directly before counting on any one restaurant or shop
